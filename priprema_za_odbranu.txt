How to use this file
- Each chapter has: WHAT we did, WHY, and LIKELY QUESTIONS with short answers.
- Numbers to remember are marked with [#].
- Part B at the end covers general course theory the professor can ask about.
- Part C lists the known weak spots in the project, with a prepared answer for each.
- Golden rule for "why not X?": every choice should rest on one of three reasons:
    (1) it would cause DATA LEAKAGE (using information the participant did not have),
    (2) the DATA does not support it (too many missing values, free text, etc.),
    (3) it is OUTSIDE THE COURSE / not needed (we keep simple, explainable methods).

---------------------------------------------------------------------
1. INTRODUCTION (Uvod i cilj rada)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT
- Data: Speed Dating Experiment, Columbia University, 2002-2004.
  Paper: Fisman, Iyengar, Kamenica, Simonson (2006), "Gender Differences in Mate
  Selection: Evidence From a Speed Dating Experiment", Quarterly Journal of Economics.
  Source: Kaggle.
- [#] 551 participants, 21 events (waves), 8,378 rows, 195 columns.
- [#] Age 18-55, median 26.
- Participants are graduate students (master, PhD, professional programs) from MANY
  schools of Columbia: business, law, medicine, engineering... NOT one faculty.
  That is why the age range goes up to 55.
- Goal: predict dec (1 = wants to see the partner again). This is binary classification.
- [#] 42% "yes", 58% "no".

HOW AN EVENT WORKED
- Before: a questionnaire (age, race, field, interests, and 100 points split across
  6 traits: attr, sinc, intel, fun, amb, shar).
- Women sat at tables and men rotated. Each date lasted 4 minutes.
- After each date: yes/no plus ratings 1-10 for the same 6 traits, plus like and prob.
- Both said yes -> match -> contact details were exchanged.
- Later questionnaires: the next day (_2) and 3-4 weeks later (_3).

HYPOTHESES (all at alpha = 0.05)
- H1: men say "yes" more often than women.          [from the original paper]
- H2: attractiveness matters more for men.           [from the original paper]
- H3: same race / smaller age gap -> more "yes".    [race: the authors' follow-up paper; age: ours]
- H4: stated preferences differ from real decisions. [ours, built on the dataset design]
- H5: hypergamy (women prefer higher-status partners more). [ours]

LIKELY QUESTIONS
Q: Why these hypotheses?
A: Each is motivated, can be tested with our columns, and uses a course method.
   H1 and H2 replicate the original paper, which checks that our pipeline is correct.
   H3 is the common "similarity" belief (homophily). H4 uses the dataset's unique
   before/after design. H5 tests a well-known claim and we report a null result honestly.
Q: Why alpha = 0.05?
A: It's a convention (Fisher) and the assignment requires it. It is the probability of a
   type I error we accept. A smaller alpha means fewer false positives but more missed
   effects (type II errors). A larger alpha means the opposite. Our key results are
   p < 0.001, so they hold at 0.01 too. We also report effect sizes.
Q: Why not test more hypotheses?
A: Multiple testing. With 10 tests at 0.05, P(at least one false positive) is about
   1 - 0.95^10 = 40%. Also each hypothesis must be motivated (no "fishing"), and the
   report was already long.
Q: H1/H2 are directional, but you used two-sided tests?
A: Two-sided is stricter. The direction is visible in the percentages and coefficients.

2. DATA DESCRIPTION AND COLUMN SELECTION (Opis i priprema podataka)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
STRUCTURE (the most important idea in the whole project)
- One row = one date seen from ONE participant's side. Every date appears twice
  (once per person). [#] 8,378 rows = about 4,200 dates.
- iid = unique person id across the whole dataset. id = number only within one event.
  ALWAYS use iid.
- pid = the partner's iid.
- Consequences:
  1. Rows are NOT independent (one person = 5 to 22 rows). This matters for tests.
  2. The train/test split must be BY PERSON, otherwise the model learns a person's
     rating habits and test results look unrealistically good.
  3. Person-level statistics (age, gender ratio) are computed once per iid.
- [#] 274 women, 277 men. 5 to 22 dates per person, median 16.
- [#] Wave 17: 10 women and 14 men. The men have round = 11 but only 10 rows, so a
  planned woman probably did not show up. The rows are correct, so we keep them.

SUFFIXES
- _o = about the partner, _1 = before the event, _s = middle of the event,
  _2 = the day after, _3 = 3-4 weeks later.
- The number after a trait: 1 = what you look for, 2 = what the opposite sex looks for,
  3 = how you rate yourself, 4 = what your own sex looks for, 5 = how others see you,
  7 = what really mattered in your decisions.

MISSING VALUES (first look)
- Two kinds of missing data:
  (a) a person skipped a question -> can be imputed;
  (b) the question did not exist in that wave -> the column cannot be used on the
      whole sample.
- expnum: only waves 1-5. attr4_1: from wave 6. attr5_1: from wave 10.
  attr7_2: only waves 15-21.
- [#] Only 263 of 551 returned the 3-4 week survey.
- [#] dec has 0 missing values, so no row is dropped because of an unknown target.
- Ratings: 2.4-4.2% missing; amb 8.5%; shar 12.7%.

COLUMN SELECTION: 195 -> 64 columns (3 rules)
1. TIME: only information that existed when the participant filled in the form after
   the date. Anything later = data leakage.
2. POINT OF VIEW: we model the participant's decision, so we keep the participant's own
   data and ratings, and drop what the partner wrote about them.
3. USABILITY: drop free text, columns missing in whole waves, and duplicates.

KEPT
- Target: dec. Identifiers (not model inputs): iid, pid, wave.
- Participant: gender, age, race, field_cd, career_c, imprace, imprelig, goal, date,
  go_out, exphappy, 17 interests.
- Preferences / self-rating: attr1_1-shar1_1, attr2_1-shar2_1, attr3_1-amb3_1.
- Partner / pair: age_o, race_o, samerace, int_corr.
- The date: attr, sinc, intel, fun, amb, shar, like, prob, met, order, condtn.

DROPPED AND WHY (learn this table)
- match, dec_o -> LEAKAGE. match contains our target. dec_o is the partner's decision,
  which the participant learns only the next day.
- attr_o..shar_o, like_o, prob_o, met_o -> LEAKAGE. The partner's ratings of the
  participant, which the participant never sees.
- All _2 and _3 columns, match_es -> created AFTER the decision.
- _s columns -> did not exist in the first half of the evening, about 52% missing.
- pf_o_att..pf_o_sha -> the partner's preferences, unknown to the participant.
- expnum, attr4_1.., attr5_1.. -> not asked in all waves.
- mn_sat, tuition, income -> 49-63% missing. (income is the median household income
  of the home zip code, NOT the person's income.)
- id, partner, idg, field, career -> duplicate information we already have.
- zipcode, from, undergra -> free text with hundreds of values ("from" has 270
  different entries for 551 people: "NYC", "New York", "Manhattan" = the same place).
- position, positin1, round -> table numbers / counts that say nothing about the partner.

LIKELY QUESTIONS
Q: Why keep age_o and race_o but drop other partner columns?
A: The participant can see or hear them during the 4 minutes, and we need them for H3.
Q: Did you check same region / field / race before dropping partner data?
A: Race: yes, samerace is kept and tested in H3. Field: yes, ista_oblast is built from
   the partner's field via pid (chapter 8). Region: from/zipcode are messy free text,
   so they cannot be compared reliably between two people.
Q: Are like and prob leakage?
A: No. They are filled in at the same moment as the decision. But like ("how much did
   you like this person") measures almost the same thing as dec, so the main models
   exclude them, and we show a separate benchmark model with them.
Q: int_corr / ista_oblast / partner career come from the PARTNER's questionnaire.
   Doesn't that break your rule 2?
A: Rule 2 is about what the partner WROTE ABOUT THE PARTICIPANT (their ratings and
   decision). Field of study, career plans and shared interests are things you learn in
   the conversation itself. They are facts about the partner that the participant
   observes, not hidden information.
Q: The same date appears twice. A in train and B in test means the same date is on
   both sides. Is that leakage?
A: Not target leakage. B's decision exists only in B's own row, and we dropped dec_o,
   so A's row never contains B's answer. The two rows have different raters, different
   ratings and different targets.

3. CLEANING (Čišćenje podataka)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT
- Duplicates: no full duplicates and no repeated (iid, pid) pairs. A pair (21,40) and
  (40,21) is NOT a duplicate: two different people, two decisions.
- [#] 10 rows in wave 5 have no pid (one partner without an iid). Kept, because dec
  and the ratings are fine.
- Types: 8 coded categorical columns were read as numbers -> converted to factors with
  labels (gender, race, race_o, goal, field_cd, career_c, condtn, samerace).
  We checked that the NA count did not change after conversion (no code got lost).
- Ordinal: date, go_out (1 = several times a week ... 7 = almost never). Kept numeric.
- Ratings 1-10 are technically ordinal but kept numeric: a 10-point scale is wide
  enough and this is standard practice.
- met: the documentation says 1 = yes, 2 = no, but half the waves used 0 for "no".
  0 and 2 -> "Ne", 1 -> "Da", other codes (3-8, 8 entries) -> NA.
- [#] 515 values outside the 1-10 scale -> NA (zeros in ratings, e.g. shar 59 rows,
  prob 49; gaming = 14, reading = 13; imprace = 0).
- Preference sums: points must sum to 100. [#] 9 people (question 1) and 19
  (question 2) deviated, so they were proportionally rescaled to 100.
- No row was deleted during cleaning. Imputation happens only after the train/test split.

LIKELY QUESTIONS
Q: Why NA and not cap 13/14 to 10?
A: We don't know whether the person meant 10 or it was a typo. Treating it as missing
   is more honest than inventing a value.
Q: Why not impute now?
A: Imputing on the whole dataset would let test data influence the medians, which is
   leakage. The medians are learned on training data only.
Q: Why are nominal codes converted to factors?
A: Otherwise the model treats code 8 (business) as "8 times more" than code 1 (law),
   which is meaningless. Factors become one-hot dummy columns.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
4. MISSING VALUES (Nedostajuće vrednosti)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT
- Chi-square test: is "rating missing" related to dec?
- [#] With a complete rating: about 43% "yes". shar missing: 32.1%. fun missing: 19.2%.
  prob missing: 14%. All p < 0.001.
- Conclusion: MNAR-like behaviour. People skip ratings when they don't like the partner.
- Decisions:
  1. Do not delete rows. We would lose about a quarter of the data, and mostly "no"
     rows, which would distort the class balance.
  2. Impute after the split (median for numeric, mode for factors, on train only).
  3. Keep the signal with broj_na_ocena (number of skipped ratings, chapter 8).

THEORY YOU MUST KNOW
- MCAR: missing completely at random (no relation to anything).
- MAR: missingness depends on OTHER observed variables.
- MNAR: missingness depends on the missing value itself, or on unobserved information.
- Why median and not mean: the median is robust to skew and outliers.
- Listwise deletion is fine only for MCAR. With MNAR it biases the data.

LIKELY QUESTIONS
Q: How do you know it's MNAR and not MAR?
A: Strictly, MNAR cannot be proven from the data alone. We showed that missingness is
   strongly tied to the decision (the same moment as the missing rating), which is
   consistent with "I didn't like them, so I didn't bother". That is why we treat it as
   informative and keep a missingness feature.
Q: Why not multiple imputation?
A: Outside the course. We tried regression imputation as an iteration and it gave the
   same AUC (0.839 vs 0.839), so the simple median is enough.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
5. OUTLIERS (Ekstremne vrednosti)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT
- Age is the only truly open scale.
- [#] IQR = 4, so the bounds are 18-34. 13 people above 34 (8 women, 5 men, ages
  35-55), 173 dates. All KEPT.
- z-score (|z| > 3) flags only 4 people (38, 39, 42, 55).
- IQR on the other columns flags values in 29 columns, but they are all valid answers
  on bounded scales (1-10, 0-100). IQR is just narrow when people answer similarly.

LIKELY QUESTIONS
Q: Why keep the outliers?
A: They are real people. Older graduate students are normal (PhD, MBA). Deleting them
   would lose 173 dates for no reason. The age effect is softened by razlika_godina.
Q: Why IQR and not z-score?
A: z-score uses the mean and sd, which the outliers themselves inflate, and it assumes
   a normal distribution. Age has a long right tail. IQR is based on quartiles, so it is
   robust.
Q: IQR rule?
A: Outlier if x < Q1 - 1.5*IQR or x > Q3 + 1.5*IQR, where IQR = Q3 - Q1.

KEY FINDINGS
- 42% yes / 58% no: a mild imbalance, so we also use F1 and AUC, not only accuracy.
- Men 47.4% yes vs women 36.5%.
- [#] Rating gap between yes and no: attr 1.91, like 1.90, shar 1.71, fun 1.62.
  sinc, intel, amb only 0.66-0.73.
- [#] Correlation with dec: attr 0.487 (the highest of the six ratings).
- Ratings are moderately to strongly correlated with each other (multicollinearity).
- What people SAY they want (intelligence, sincerity) differs from what decides.
- Idea of one global rating -> prosek_ocena, ponderisana_ocena in chapter 8.

LIKELY QUESTIONS
Q: Why do sinc/intel/amb matter so little?
A: They are hard to judge in 4 minutes. Visible things (looks, fun, shared interests)
   decide a first impression.
Q: Was EDA done on all data, including the test set? Isn't that snooping?
A: Yes, EDA used all data. But no model choice (lambda, k, threshold, imputation) was
   made with the test set. Those were chosen by cross-validation on train only. The
   EDA findings are strong effects that don't depend on which 110 people ended up in
   the test set.

7. STATISTICAL TESTS (Statistički testovi)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
GENERAL
- Rows are not independent, so row-level p-values are a bit too small. We handle this
  by: (1) the H1 test per person, (2) always reporting effect sizes, which don't depend
  on n, (3) being careful with borderline results.
- Mixed models (random effects per person) are outside the course. This is stated in
  the report.
- Large n (8,378) means tiny differences become "significant", hence the effect sizes.

H1 - men say yes more
- [#] Chi-square = 101.36, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.11 (small). 47.4% vs 36.5%.
- Per person: Shapiro-Wilk rejects normality (about 9% of people said yes to nobody or
  to everyone), so Mann-Whitney is used instead of the t-test. p < 0.001.
- The per-person test is the more reliable one (independent observations).
Q: Why Mann-Whitney? A: The data is not normal, so it's the non-parametric alternative
   to the t-test. It compares ranks.

H2 - attractiveness matters more for men
- glm(dec ~ attr * gender, binomial). The interaction is the test.
- [#] Interaction OR = 1.19, p < 0.001. Each extra point of attr multiplies the odds
  of "yes" by 2.17 for men vs 1.82 for women.
Q: Why logistic regression and not chi-square?
A: attr is a numeric scale and we need to compare SLOPES between genders. That is an
   interaction term.
Q: What is an odds ratio?
A: odds = p/(1-p). OR = exp(coefficient) = how many times the odds change for +1 unit.

H3 - same race, age gap
- Race: [#] chi-square = 4.35, p = 0.037, V = 0.023 (negligible). 43.4% vs 41.1%.
- Age: skewness 1.82 (not normal), Shapiro can't be used for n > 5000, so a Q-Q plot.
  Mann-Whitney p = 0.00013, probability of superiority 0.525 (about 0.5 = no effect).
  Both medians = 3 years.
- Conclusion: formally significant (large n), practically negligible.
Q: Why didn't you use Shapiro for age?
A: R's shapiro.test accepts at most 5000 observations. We used a Q-Q plot and skewness.
Q: What is the probability of superiority?
A: P(a random "no" date has a larger age gap than a random "yes" date). 0.5 = no effect.

H4 - stated vs revealed preferences
- Main check: a logistic regression per gender on the 6 STANDARDIZED ratings. The rank
  of the coefficients (revealed) is compared with the rank of the pre-event points
  (stated).
  [#] Women: intelligence 1st in the questionnaire (21 points), but coefficient only
      0.19 (4th). attr coefficient 0.81 (1st). shar jumps from last to 2nd (0.59).
  [#] Men: attr is 1st in both (27.2 points, coefficient 1.25). intel is 2nd in the
      questionnaire but coefficient -0.04 (CI includes 0).
- Supplementary check: paired Wilcoxon, attr1_1 vs attr7_2, waves 15-21, n = 123.
  [#] attr 23.9 -> 32.2 points. intel 20.1 -> 15.8, sinc 17.0 -> 14.2, amb 9.2 -> 7.4,
      all p < 0.001. fun, shar p about 0.65 (no change).
- BUG WE FIXED: previously every p-value was 1, because the test ran on the two rounded
  means instead of the 123 pairs. If asked: "We found it, the test now runs on all
  123 pairs, and the conclusion stayed the same."
Q: Why standardize the ratings in H4?
A: So the coefficients are on the same scale and can be ranked. Each coefficient is the
   effect of +1 standard deviation.
Q: Why do sinc and amb have negative coefficients?
A: Multicollinearity. Holding attr/fun/shar fixed, a high sinc alone often means a
   "polite high rating" given to someone the person rejected.
Q: Why paired Wilcoxon?
A: The same person answers twice (before/after), so the samples are dependent, and the
   differences are not normal.
Q: Ranking comparison is not a formal test?
A: Correct, it is descriptive. The confidence intervals (attr 0.69-0.92 vs intel
   0.05-0.33 for women do not overlap) and the Wilcoxon test are the formal part.

H5 - hypergamy
- Status = the partner's planned career (law, medicine, finance/banking = high),
  read via pid from the partner's questionnaire.
- Classic hypergamy by education can't be tested: everyone is a Columbia grad student.
  income is a zip-code median and 49% missing, so it is not used.
- [#] Women: 38.0% yes to high status vs 34.7%. Men: 55.3% vs 44.6%.
  Interaction OR = 1.33 (95% CI 1.11-1.61), p = 0.003, in FAVOUR OF MEN (the opposite
  of hypergamy).
- Controlling for attr, fun, shar: the effect disappears for women (p = 0.88) but
  stays for men (interaction p = 0.032). Men rated high-status women as more attractive
  (6.76 vs 6.35).
- Conclusion: NOT confirmed.
Q: Is a null result a problem?
A: No. It is honest science. We tested the claim, and the data does not support it.
Q: Limitations? A: career is planned, not actual; similar education for everyone;
   4 minutes is not choosing a spouse; row-level p-values are slightly optimistic.

MULTIPLE TESTING
- 5 hypotheses. Bonferroni: 0.05/5 = 0.01. All main results survive, except race in H3
  (p = 0.037), which we already call negligible.

8. FEATURE ENGINEERING (Nove promenljive)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
NEW COLUMNS (and why)
- razlika_godina = |age - age_o|: H3, and it softens the extreme ages.
- stariji_partner = age_o - age: the DIRECTION of the age gap (non-linear pattern).
- prosek_ocena = mean of the 6 ratings: one global rating.
- attr_relativno = attr minus the person's mean attr over the evening: removes the
  difference between strict and lenient raters. [#] r = 0.448. Bottom quintile 10.5%
  yes, top quintile 73.7%.
- ponderisana_ocena = ratings weighted by the person's own 100-point preferences: how
  well the partner fits the stated criteria.
- relativni_redosled = order / number of dates (0-1): a fatigue effect. Result: none
  (40.4-43% yes in all quarters of the evening).
- broj_na_ocena = number of skipped ratings (0-8): keeps the MNAR signal.
  [#] 0 skipped: 43.5%, 4-7 skipped: 25.4%, all 8 skipped: 2.1% yes.
- ista_oblast = participant and partner study the same field.
  [#] 48.5% vs 41.3%, chi-square = 18.13, p < 0.001. Only 960 dates (about 12%).
  Still holds when controlling for field_cd and int_corr (OR 1.28, p = 0.0004), so it
  is not just "business students are many".
- Rare categories: field_cd and career_c levels with < 10 PARTICIPANTS merged into
  "Ostalo". [#] field_cd 18 -> 13 levels, career_c 17 -> 11 (11 fewer dummy columns).
- Global ratings alone: [#] AUC prosek_ocena 0.759, ponderisana_ocena 0.765, attr 0.789.

LIKELY QUESTIONS
Q: Why merge rare categories? Why 10?
A: With a split by person, a level with 1-3 people may end up only in the test set,
   and a dummy column that is 1 for 1-3 people describes those individuals, not a
   pattern (noise, overfitting). 10 is a simple rule so each level appears in both train
   and test. It does not use dec, so it is not leakage.
Q: Counted on all data, including test?
A: Only the number of people per category was counted, never the target. That is
   harmless, but it could also be done on train only.
Q: Is attr_relativno leakage?
A: It doesn't use dec. But it uses the mean over the whole evening, so for early dates
   it includes later ratings. That means the model works in a "end of the evening, all
   forms collected" scenario. This is stated as a limitation.
Q: prosek_ocena has VIF 72.7. Why keep it?
A: It is almost a linear combination of the 6 ratings. That hurts coefficient
   interpretation in plain logistic regression, not prediction. Lasso/ridge handle
   collinearity.
Q: Why compute broj_na_ocena before imputation?
A: After imputation the missing values are filled in and the information is gone.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
9. MACHINE LEARNING (Mašinsko učenje)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
SPLIT
- By PARTICIPANT (iid), 80/20, set.seed(2026).
- [#] Train: 441 people, 6,732 rows, 42.7% yes. Test: 110 people, 1,646 rows, 39.1% yes.
  Overlap of people = 0.
Q: Why by person? A: Otherwise the same person is in both train and test, the model
   learns their personal habits, and the results are unrealistically good.
Q: Why 80/20? A: A common convention. 110 test people is enough for a stable estimate,
   and the bootstrap CI shows the remaining uncertainty.
Q: Why seed 2026? A: Reproducibility. The number itself is arbitrary.

PREPARATION - pripremi() (learned on TRAIN ONLY, then applied to test)
1. Imputation: median (numeric), mode (factors).
2. One-hot encoding with model.matrix, dropping the first (reference) level, which
   avoids the dummy-variable trap (perfect collinearity with the intercept).
3. Standardization (mean 0, sd 1) with TRAIN means and sds. Constant columns removed.
[#] X_tr = 6,732 x 96 columns.

SCALING - which models? ALL of them, because they all use pripremi(). But it matters
differently:
- kNN: ESSENTIAL (Euclidean distance; otherwise 0-100 point columns dominate 1-10 ratings).
- ridge/lasso: needed so the penalty treats all coefficients equally (glmnet also
  standardizes internally).
- logistic regression: predictions do NOT change, only the coefficients become
  comparable.
- baseline: no effect.

METRICS
- accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1, AUC. AUC is the main metric.
- AUC = P(a random "yes" date gets a higher probability than a random "no" date).
  It is the same as the Mann-Whitney statistic, which is why it is computed via ranks.
  0.5 = random, 1 = perfect. It does not depend on the threshold.
- F1 = harmonic mean of precision and recall. Useful with imbalanced classes.
Q: Why not only accuracy? A: With 58% "no", a model that always says "no" has 58%
   accuracy and is useless.

CROSS-VALIDATION
- 5-fold, folds BY PERSON, on train only. pripremi() is re-learned inside every fold.
- Inside cv.glmnet: another inner 5-fold CV by person to choose lambda (nested).
- The test set is untouched until the end.

MODELS
- Baseline: always predicts the training share of "yes" (0.427 < 0.5, so always "no").
- Logistic regression (glm): basic parametric, interpretable model.
- Ridge (alpha = 0): penalty lambda * sum(beta^2). Shrinks coefficients, helps with
  multicollinearity, never exactly 0.
- Lasso (alpha = 1): penalty lambda * sum(|beta|). Sets some coefficients to EXACTLY 0,
  which gives automatic feature selection.
- kNN: the share of "yes" among the k nearest dates (Euclidean). Non-parametric.
Q: Why not linear regression? A: For a 0/1 target it predicts values outside [0, 1],
   which can't be probabilities. Logistic regression models log(p/(1-p)).
Q: Why not trees / random forest / SVM? A: Not in the course syllabus, and we want
   simple, interpretable models. Linear models already do well because "yes" grows
   roughly monotonically with the ratings.
Q: Why does lasso give exact zeros and ridge doesn't? A: The L1 penalty has "corners"
   at zero (the geometry of the |beta| diamond), so the optimum often lands exactly on
   an axis. L2 (a circle) only shrinks.

CV RESULTS [#]
- Lasso 0.839 (accuracy 76.0%, F1 0.706), Ridge 0.834, Logistic 0.819 (75.1%),
  kNN (k = 51) 0.772, baseline accuracy 57.3%.
- Baseline CV AUC shows 0.47 instead of 0.50: each fold predicts a different constant,
  and pooling the 5 folds ranks the folds against each other. Not a bug.
- With like/prob: logistic 0.819 -> 0.844, lasso 0.839 -> 0.858. A MODERATE
  improvement (about 2 points), so like adds some information but not much.

10. ITERATIONS (Iteracije modela)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
- lambda: [#] lasso lambda.min = 0.0178, CV AUC 0.84. A small lambda overfits, a large
  lambda removes useful columns (the bias-variance trade-off).
- k for kNN: [#] the grid 5-151. With all 96 columns kNN is weak (curse of
  dimensionality). With only 13 columns (6 ratings + 7 new) and k = 101: AUC 0.831.
  kNN (k = 51, all columns) 0.772 -> 0.831 after narrowing.
  Q: Why odd k? A: No tied votes between 2 classes.
  Q: Small vs large k? A: small k = flexible, low bias, high variance; large k =
     smooth, higher bias, low variance.
- Lasso coefficients: [#] keeps 17 of 96 (79 set to 0). Top: attr_relativno 0.519,
  shar 0.419, attr 0.398, fun 0.328, broj_na_ocena -0.230.
- VIF: [#] prosek_ocena 72.7. Without it, the 6 ratings have VIF 1.6-3.9 and
  ponderisana_ocena 12.7. VIF = 1/(1 - R^2) of that predictor regressed on the others.
  > 5 or > 10 = a problem. Don't put all 6 preference points in one VIF check: they sum
  to 100 (perfect collinearity).
- attr^2 + attr x gender: [#] AUC 0.819 -> 0.821. A statistically significant effect
  (H2) is not the same as better prediction.
- Imputation: median 0.839 vs regression 0.839, so we keep the simple median.
  broj_na_ocena already carries the missingness signal.
- Threshold: [#] lasso CV specificity 82.3% vs recall 67.5% at 0.5. Best F1 = 0.736 at
  threshold 0.35 (chosen in CV, NOT on test). This is how we handle class imbalance
  (instead of oversampling).

Q: Logistic beat lasso on test. Why do you call lasso the main model?
A: The model was chosen BEFORE the test, by CV. Switching after looking at the test
   would turn the test set into another validation set and the estimate would no longer
   be honest. Also the difference (0.005) is far smaller than the bootstrap CI width,
   and lasso uses only 17 columns.
Q: Why bootstrap by person and not by row? A: To keep the dependence between rows of
   the same person (the same reason as the split).
Q: What does the percentile CI mean? A: The 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of the 1000
   bootstrap AUCs.

=====================================================================
PART B - COURSE THEORY HE CAN ASK (the oral exam covers the whole course)
=====================================================================
- Data types: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio. Our examples: race (nominal),
  go_out (ordinal), age (ratio).
- Anomalies: IQR rule, z-score; error vs genuine extreme value.
- MCAR / MAR / MNAR; deletion vs imputation (mean, median, mode, regression).
- One-hot encoding; the reference level; the dummy-variable trap.
- Statistical learning: prediction vs inference; parametric vs non-parametric.
- Bias-variance trade-off: flexible models have low bias and high variance;
  test error = bias^2 + variance + irreducible error.
- Overfitting: good on train, bad on new data. Fix it with CV and regularization.
- kNN: distance, the choice of k, scaling, the curse of dimensionality.
- Linear regression: least squares, interpretation of coefficients, R^2, interactions,
  polynomial terms, VIF / multicollinearity, AIC/BIC (penalize complexity; BIC
  penalizes more).
- Logistic regression: logit = log(p/(1-p)), odds ratio = exp(beta), maximum likelihood.
- Train/test, validation set, k-fold CV, LOOCV (n folds; low bias, high variance,
  expensive).
- Bootstrap: resampling WITH replacement, used to estimate the SE / CI of a statistic.
- Ridge / lasso / elastic net (alpha between 0 and 1); the role of lambda;
  lambda.min vs lambda.1se (the simplest model within 1 SE of the best).
- PCR / PCA: components = linear combinations of maximum variance. We did NOT use it,
  because lasso keeps interpretable original columns.
- Clustering (k-means, hierarchical): we did not use it, because our task is supervised.
- Hypothesis tests: H0/H1, p-value (P(data this extreme | H0), NOT P(H0 true)),
  type I/II errors, power, parametric vs non-parametric, normality checks
  (Shapiro, Q-Q plot), chi-square, t-test vs Mann-Whitney, paired tests, effect size.
